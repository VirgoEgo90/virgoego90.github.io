Budgeting guide

The 50/30/20 budget rule, explained

By Ivan Dummett · Updated November 18, 2026

Open the free 50/30/20 calculator

The 50/30/20 rule is one of the simplest ways to sketch a budget. You take the money that actually lands in your account each month and split it three ways: about half for needs, about a third for wants, and a fifth for savings and paying down debt faster. It was popularized by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi in their 2005 book All Your Worth, and it has stuck around because it is easy to remember.

This guide walks through the three buckets, shows the math, and covers what to do when your real life doesn't fit the percentages. It is a way of organizing numbers, not financial advice.

Start with take-home pay, not salary

The rule works on after-tax income — what arrives after taxes and any automatic deductions. If your pay varies, use a low-but-realistic month rather than your best one. If retirement contributions or health premiums come out of your paycheck before you see it, you can either add them back in (and count them in the right bucket) or leave everything as-is and work with the number you receive. Pick one approach and stay consistent.

The three buckets

50% — needs

Things you would have to pay even in a tight month: rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, insurance, transportation to work, minimum debt payments, and childcare. If skipping it would cause a real problem, it is probably a need.

30% — wants

Everything that makes life nicer but could be paused: eating out, streaming services, hobbies, travel, upgrades. Gifts and holidays usually live here too, which is why December can throw the plan off.

20% — savings and extra debt payments

Emergency savings, sinking funds for known costs, retirement contributions you make yourself, and anything you pay on a debt above the minimum.

A worked example

Say your take-home pay is $3,800 a month:

  • Needs: 50% × $3,800 = $1,900
  • Wants: 30% × $3,800 = $1,140
  • Savings and extra debt: 20% × $3,800 = $760

When the numbers don't fit

In many cities, rent alone can take more than half of take-home pay. That doesn't mean the rule is useless — it means you adjust it. Common variations people use:

  • 60/20/20 when fixed costs are high and you want to protect savings.
  • 70/20/10 as a temporary plan while you rebuild after a big expense.
  • 50/20/30 flipped toward savings when you are working on a specific goal.

The point is to make the split visible. Once you can see where the money goes, you can decide what to change.

Turning the split into a monthly habit

  1. Write down your take-home pay for the month.
  2. List fixed needs first, then the variable ones like groceries and gas.
  3. Give each want category a limit that fits inside your wants number.
  4. Move the savings amount on payday, before it gets spent.
  5. At the end of the month, compare what you planned with what happened.

That last step is where a spreadsheet earns its keep. A tracker that totals categories for you makes the monthly check-in take minutes instead of an evening.

Frequently asked questions

Is debt a need or savings?

The required minimum payment is a need. Anything extra you pay counts toward the 20%.

Do I have to hit the percentages exactly?

No. Treat them as a starting point and adjust them to fit your life.

Does it work with irregular income?

Yes, if you base it on a cautious monthly figure and save extra in good months.
