Planning guide

How to set up an ADHD-friendly paper planner

By Ivan Dummett · Updated November 18, 2026

Print a free monthly habit trackerFree ADHD planner daily page

Many people with ADHD buy a planner in January and lose it by February. That doesn't mean planners don't work for you. It usually means the planner asked for too much. This guide sets up a simple paper system that's easy to come back to after you miss a day or a week.

This is about organizing paper, not medical advice. If you're looking for diagnosis or treatment, talk to a qualified professional.

Principle 1: undated beats dated

A dated planner shows every skipped day as a blank page. An undated one doesn't. Write today's date at the top and carry on. If you love dated calendars, keep one monthly calendar for appointments and make everything else undated.

Principle 2: fewer pages, used more often

Start with three page types:

  1. A weekly overview: appointments, deadlines, and three things that would make the week feel successful.
  2. A daily page: today's three, a rough schedule, and a box to dump everything else.
  3. A brain dump page: a place to get every loose thought out of your head so it stops circling.

Add trackers, routines, and cleaning lists later, only if you keep missing them.

The daily "today's three"

A to-do list with 22 items is a wish list. Pick three things for today, at most. Make at least one of them small enough to finish in ten minutes. Everything else goes in the brain dump or a "if I have time" list. Crossing off three things feels better than leaving nineteen unfinished.

The brain dump

Set a timer for five minutes and write down everything: tasks, worries, ideas, things to buy. Don't sort while you write. When the timer ends, circle anything with a deadline, move one or two items to today's three, and leave the rest. It's on paper now, so you don't have to keep remembering it.

A dopamine menu

A dopamine menu is a list of activities you enjoy, sorted by how much time and energy they take:

  • Starters (5 minutes): a song, a short walk, a stretch, a favorite snack.
  • Mains (30–60 minutes): a hobby, a class, cooking something new.
  • Sides: things to pair with a boring task, like a podcast or a body-double video.
  • Desserts: easy treats to use carefully, like scrolling or a show.

When you're stuck, check the menu instead of reaching for your phone.

Make the planner hard to lose

  • Keep it in one spot — open on the kitchen counter or next to the keys.
  • Clip a pen to it.
  • Pair a planner check with something you already do, like morning coffee.
  • Use a binder or clipboard so you can print only the pages you'll use this week.

When you fall off

You will skip days, and that's fine. Don't try to catch up. Start a fresh daily page, do a five-minute brain dump, and pick today's three. That's the whole restart.
